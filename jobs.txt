• Outsourced activities must be properly identified and classified.
• Vendor selection should be supported by adequate due diligence and documentation.

• Board-approved outsourcing/vendor-management frameworks must be effectively implemented.
• Internal controls should ensure proper monitoring of outsourced activities.

The larger message is clear:
A compliance policy on paper is not enough. Regulators increasingly expect evidence of implementation, monitoring and Board oversight.

Compliance is a continuous process — not a one-time exercise.

🔹 Good compliance is not about saying no. It is about helping the business understand risk well enough to make a sound decision. 

Before Compliance says “no,” there are a few questions worth asking.

→ What is the business trying to accomplish?

→ What is the specific risk, not just the rule or requirement?

→ How likely is that risk to occur, and what would the impact really be?

→ Can we change the process, add a control, or narrow the scope enough to make the risk manageable?

Compliance has an important role in identifying risk, translating requirements, and helping the business understand the potential consequences of its choices.

But that doesn’t mean every risk belongs to Compliance or that every risk requires a “no.”

The strongest compliance programs create guardrails that help people make better decisions without unnecessarily slowing the business down.

That distinction matters.

Because a technically compliant process can still be a poorly designed process.

And mature compliance programs should make doing the right thing easier,  not harder.

Stakeholder Management: What Nobody Tells You

People often talk about stakeholder management as though it means keeping everyone happy.

My experience has taught me something different.

⚖️ Sometimes, stakeholder management means managing competing expectations while protecting what matters most.

In my role, I sit at an interesting intersection.

On one side is the regulator, with clear regulatory requirements that financial service providers must meet.

Then there is management, who have to balance business priorities, client needs and the institution’s regulatory obligations.

There are Relationship Managers who work closely with clients and understand the urgency and commercial realities behind their requests.

There is Compliance, whose role is to challenge and test whether the right controls are in place.

And then there are the clients — the very reason financial service providers exist: to serve their financial needs.

Each stakeholder comes with different expectations, priorities and pressures.

And this is where the real complexity of reconciling and aligning diverse stakeholder expectations begins.

While a client may have a genuine business need, every transaction must still comply with applicable regulatory requirements.

The business may want a practical solution.

Compliance may be asking questions.

And somewhere in the middle, I still have to ask:
“Does this meet the applicable regulatory and control requirements?”

My role is not to choose between the client, the business, Compliance or the regulator.

🧭 It is to understand the different expectations, identify the risks, and help find a solution within the regulatory and control framework.

Sometimes that means explaining why something cannot be done.

Sometimes it means finding an alternative route.

Sometimes it means escalating an issue when the risk cannot be resolved at my level.

Other times, that means having a difficult conversation with someone more senior.

But regardless of who is asking, the principle remains the same:

🛡️ Stakeholder management should never mean compromising the control environment.

It means managing expectations while protecting the institution and the client.

That has been one of the most valuable leadership lessons of my career:

You don't always need authority to influence outcomes.

Sometimes you need judgement.

Sometimes you need courage.

Sometimes you need diplomacy.

And othertimes, you need the confidence to be comfortable being the person in the middle.

Because in a regulated environment, the easiest answer isn't always the right answer.

Compliance Is Strongest When It Is Understood


A policy can be perfectly written and still fail.
Why?...   Because compliance does not happen when a policy is published. It happens when people understand what is expected and apply it consistently.

Many organisations invest heavily in policies, procedures and training but sometimes overlook the most important question:
Can employees translate the requirement into the right decision at the right moment?

Leadership Insight:
The effectiveness of compliance depends not only on the quality of the framework, but on the quality of decisions it produces.

A stronger approach is to:
• Convert complex requirements into practical guidance
• Use real-life scenarios instead of only policy language
• Clearly define decision-making responsibilities
• Encourage employees to ask questions early
• Measure understanding—not just training completion
• Learn from recurring errors and near misses

A “100% training completion” metric may look impressive.
But the real test is what happens when an employee faces a difficult decision without someone from Compliance standing beside them.
That is where compliance culture becomes visible.

Does your organisation measure compliance awareness—or only compliance activity?

Compliance Should Join the Product Discussion Before Launch Day
A feature is designed, development is nearly complete, and the launch date is announced only then is Compliance asked to approve it.
At that stage, even a valid concern can look like an obstacle. Time and money have been invested, and every change feels expensive.
Early compliance involvement does not mean controlling product design. It means identifying legal, regulatory and operational questions while the solution is flexible.
Before development moves too far, ask:
• Who will use the product, and where?
 • How will money, data and instructions move?
 • Which customers and third parties require due diligence?
 • What fees, limitations and complaint routes must be disclosed?
 • Which monitoring, testing and approvals are needed?
Consider a payment-link feature allowing merchants to use additional websites. If reviewed after launch, the business may discover that the new channel was never assessed, merchant activity is missing from the risk profile, and monitoring rules cannot identify related transactions.
Those issues are easier to solve during design than after customers are active.
Product governance should define when Compliance is consulted, what information the product owner provides, who accepts unresolved risks, and what evidence is required before launch. Material changes after approval should trigger another review.
Takeaway: Compliance creates more value when it helps shape a safe route to market not when it receives a finished product for immediate approval.

I’ve seen many such posts recently where companies are hiring for an “HR Manager” role with a budget of just ₹50–55K per month, while expecting the person to manage almost the entire HR function.
 
The expectations often include end-to-end recruitment, onboarding, payroll, employee engagement, performance management, HR operations, compliance, policies, and employee relations — basically, the responsibilities of multiple HR roles under one designation.
 
I completely understand that in a growing organization, HR professionals have to wear multiple hats. I’ve experienced this myself and believe it can be a great learning opportunity.
 
But at some point, we also need to ask: Are the responsibilities, expectations, authority, and compensation aligned?

If you want someone to independently own the complete HR function and contribute to business growth, the role needs to be valued accordingly.

HR is not just an administrative function. It directly impacts hiring, retention, culture, employee experience, and ultimately business performance.

Maybe it’s time we start looking beyond the designation and ask whether the scope of work matches the compensation being offered.

DIGITAL BANKING & COMPLIANCE
Part 11 - Banking Compliance Is Also About Process Design
 

A Good Compliance Control Can Still Fail If the Process Around It Is Poor.

Imagine a fraud alert is generated.
But nobody knows:
Who should investigate it?
How quickly?
What information should they review?
What decision should they make?
Where should the decision be recorded?
What happens next?

The technology may be working perfectly.
The process is not.

This is why compliance should be viewed as:
People + Process + Technology + Data

Example: Fraud Alert Process
Transaction occurs
↓
Risk engine generates alert
↓
Alert is prioritised
↓
Analyst investigates
↓
Decision recorded
↓
Customer communication, if required
↓
Case closed / escalated
↓
Audit trail maintained

A BA can improve this process by identifying:
🔹 Process bottlenecks
🔹 Duplicate activities
🔹 Manual handoffs
🔹 Missing ownership
🔹 SLA issues
🔹 Data gaps
🔹 Exception scenarios

Then the BA can translate improvements into:
Process maps + Requirements + User Stories + Acceptance Criteria + KPIs

Compliance should not create unnecessary operational complexity.
The objective should be:
Effective control + efficient process + positive customer experience.
